When a merchant's payment capture will not resolve before the order's ship-by date, an agent has to choose without knowing. FinalityBench's strongest policy escalates to a human, which is safe and, on that corpus, never the best available action: escalation costs a flat fee on every case while the alternatives cost something that depends on the order. We replace the fixed rule with an expected-value comparison under a belief fitted from the benchmark's public split. It is a deadline decision rule, not a general sequential planner: a conditional probability and one comparison of four closed-form expressions, with no search over action sequences. Over 321 tasks and 8,025 graded episodes it is exactly correct on 91.9%\ against the gate's 85.4%, and its mean position gap on a paired resample is $-2.74 [-3.80, -1.80] lower. The margin turns on what escalation is worth, not only on what it costs. FinalityBench's analyst starts no new money movement, so escalation cannot rescue a capture that never settles. Re-scoring every episode under more capable analysts shrinks the gap from $2.74 to $0.89 without changing its sign: only 7 of the gate's 47 escalations are on cases a retry could rescue. A sweep over 27 cost settings reuses the same tasks and seeds, so it varies one experiment rather than confirming the result 27 times. Two of four components contribute nothing. The safety shield is inert because the decision rule already maximises expected value, and pursuing why gave the more transferable result: an expected-value shield cannot object to shipping without checking, because at this settle rate that bet pays, while a shield reasoning about the branch it cannot rule out cuts a ship-on-first-sign policy's pre-determination irreversible actions from 66.0%\ to 7.0%\ and is worse on the corpus's own outcome distribution.
