When a merchant's payment capture will not resolve before the order's ship-by date, an agent has to choose without knowing. The strongest policy shipped with the FinalityBench benchmark handles this by escalating to a human, which is safe and, on that corpus, never the best available action: escalation costs a flat fee on every case while the alternatives cost something that depends on the order. We replace the fixed rule with an expected-value comparison under a belief fitted from the benchmark's public split, and evaluate on 321 tasks and 8,025 graded episodes. The planner reaches 91.9%\ exactly correct against the gate's 85.4%, and on a resample that gives both policies the same tasks its mean position gap is $-2.74 [-3.80, -1.80] lower, an interval that excludes zero and amounts to a nine per cent reduction. The advantage survives every one of 27 settings of the three cost constants the benchmark stipulates, and it survives evaluation on a regenerated corpus the belief was not fitted to. An ablation is less flattering: of four components, two contribute nothing. Removing the safety shield costs $0.00 [0.00, 0.00] and removing the value-of-information rule costs $0.00 [0.00, 0.00]. The shield is inert because the planner already maximises expected value, and pursuing why produced the more interesting result. At the settle rate this corpus exhibits, shipping without checking is a bet that pays, so an expected-value shield cannot object to it. A shield that reasons about the branch it cannot rule out does object, and cuts a ship-on-first-sign policy's pre-determination irreversible actions from 66.0%\ to 7.0%\ while making it worse on the corpus's own outcome distribution. Which of those an operator wants is a question about risk preference that the benchmark's paired scoring answers one way and its natural distribution answers the other.
